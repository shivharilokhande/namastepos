Restaurant Analytics & Reports: The Numbers Owners Should Watch

Updated 28 Aug 2026·8 min read·Analytics

Most restaurant software buries you in dashboards you never open. But running a restaurant well needs only a handful of numbers, read consistently. Here are the reports that actually change decisions — and what to do with each.

Data you'll act on beats data you'll admire

A common trap is mistaking more charts for more insight. The owners who use data well don't look at everything — they look at a few numbers, often, and act. The goal of this guide is to give you that short list, so your POS reports become a morning habit rather than a monthly formality.

1. Daily & weekly sales trend

Start with the simplest view: sales by day, this week versus last. It tells you instantly whether you're growing, flat or slipping, and it surfaces patterns — the slow Tuesday, the weekend spike — that shape staffing and prep. Watch the trend line, not a single day; one bad day is noise, four in a row is a signal.

2. Best-sellers and worst-sellers

Rank your items by units sold and by revenue. Your best-sellers are your brand — protect their quality and consider featuring them harder. Your worst-sellers are a decision: promote, re-price, or cut. A quiet dish that also needs fresh, perishable prep is a double loss, and this report is how you spot it.

3. Peak hours

Sales by hour is one of the most underused reports in a restaurant. It tells you exactly when to add staff, when to prep, and when a second billing point earns its keep. Rostering to your real peaks — instead of a fixed shift pattern — cuts both queues and idle wage cost.

4. Food cost and P&L

Revenue is vanity; margin is sanity. A consolidated P&L that nets your sales against food cost, staff and expenses tells you whether all that activity actually made money. Track food-cost percentage as a trend and pair it with your inventory's theoretical-vs-actual gap to see where margin leaks.

5. Discounts, voids and refunds

Leakage often hides in the "adjustments". A rising discount rate or a spike in voids after items are fired can quietly erode margin or mask shrinkage. Review these weekly, logged to the staff member who made them — not to police honest teams, but to make the exceptions visible.

Make it a ritual, not a report dump

Pick a five-minute daily glance (yesterday's sales, today's prep from last week) and a fifteen-minute weekly review (trend, best/worst, food cost, discounts). Consistency beats depth: the owner who checks five numbers every morning will out-manage the one who runs a hundred-page report once a quarter.
